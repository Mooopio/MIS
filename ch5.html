What is IT Infrastructure?

IT Infrastructure is the foundation that supports all of a firm's information systems. Think of it like the plumbing and electrical grid of a building — invisible when it works, critical when it doesn't.

It consists of two layers:

  • Physical layer: Hardware devices, cables, servers, data centers
  • Software/Service layer: Operating systems, enterprise apps, data management, telecom services

Key insight: IT infrastructure is a service platform — it enables the firm to offer services to customers, employees, and suppliers. More infrastructure capability = more business capability.

The 6 Core Infrastructure Services

  • Computing platforms — servers, cloud, processing power
  • Telecommunications — networking, internet, communication
  • Data management — databases, storage systems
  • Application software — ERP, CRM, enterprise apps
  • Physical facilities management — data centers, cabling
  • IT management, education, and other services — IT staff, training, consulting

5 Eras of IT Infrastructure Evolution EXAM FAVORITE

Era 1: General-Purpose Mainframe & Minicomputer (1959–present)

▼

1958: IBM introduced the first mainframes — massive, room-sized computers owned by large corporations and governments. 1965: DEC introduced cheaper minicomputers, bringing computing to mid-sized businesses.

A mainframe is like a giant factory that processes everything centrally. All the "workers" (terminals) send requests to it; it does the work and sends results back.

Era 2: Personal Computer Era (1981–present)

▼

1981: IBM introduced the PC. Proliferation in the '80s and '90s led to mass adoption of personal productivity software (Word, Excel, etc.). Computing power moved to individuals' desks.

Era 3: Client/Server Era (1983–present)

▼

Desktop clients (your PC) are networked to servers. Processing is split between them. Architectures:

  • Two-tier: Client talks directly to one server
  • N-tier (multi-tier): Multiple server layers — web server → application server → database server
Like a restaurant: the customer (client) orders food. A waiter (web server) takes the order, the kitchen (application server) cooks it, and the pantry (database server) stores the ingredients.

Era 4: Enterprise Computing Era (1992–present)

▼

Companies needed to integrate all their separate systems. The Internet provided common standards (TCP/IP, HTTP) that allowed different systems to talk to each other. Enterprise applications (SAP, Oracle ERP) unified business processes.

Era 5: Cloud & Mobile Computing (2000–present)

▼

Cloud computing delivers computing power and software over the Internet on demand — like electricity from a utility. This is the fastest-growing form of computing. Mobile devices (smartphones, tablets) put computing in everyone's pocket.

Cloud = renting electricity vs. owning a generator. You pay for what you use; someone else maintains the infrastructure.

Technology Drivers of Infrastructure Evolution EXAM FAVORITE

1. Moore's Law

Computing power doubles every 18 months while costs stay the same (or fall). This exponential growth has driven the entire digital revolution. More than 2 billion transistors now fit on a single microprocessor, achieving 500,000+ MIPS (millions of instructions per second).

Nanotechnology extends Moore's Law further — shrinking transistors to sizes comparable to a virus. Nanotubes (carbon tubes 10,000× thinner than a human hair) act as microscopic wires.

2. Law of Mass Digital Storage

The amount of digital storage doubles every year while the cost per kilobyte falls exponentially. Since the first magnetic storage device (1955), data storage has become millions of times cheaper.

3. Metcalfe's Law

Network value grows exponentially with the number of members. If a network has n members, it has n(n-1)/2 potential connections. The more people join, the more valuable it becomes — driving even more people to join (network effects).

A phone network with 1 person is useless. With 2, there's 1 connection. With 10, there are 45 connections. With 1,000 — almost 500,000 connections!

4. Declining Communication Costs

As of the textbook's writing, ~2.3 billion people had Internet access. The cost per kilobit of Internet access has fallen exponentially since 1995. DSL and cable deliver a kilobit for ~2 cents retail. As costs approach zero, utilization explodes.

5. Technology Standards

Standards are agreed-upon specifications that ensure products from different manufacturers work together. They create economies of scale (manufacturers focus on one standard → mass production → lower prices) and network effects. Examples: TCP/IP, HTML, USB, Bluetooth.

Contemporary Hardware Platform Trends

Mobile Digital Platform

Smartphones (iPhone, Android, BlackBerry), netbooks, tablets (iPad), and e-readers (Kindle, Nook) are now primary computing devices for many workers. They handle web, email, IM, and data tasks.

BYOD (Bring Your Own Device): Employees use personal devices at work. This is part of the broader consumerization of IT — where technology emerges in consumer markets first, then spreads to business, forcing IT departments to rethink acquisition and management strategies.

Grid Computing

Connects geographically distributed computers into a single virtual network, combining their processing power into a virtual supercomputer. Benefits: cost savings, speed, agility. Often used for scientific research, financial modeling.

Virtualization

Software allows a single physical machine to run multiple virtual machines (each with its own OS). Benefits: fewer physical servers needed, lower energy/hardware costs, easier centralization and management.

Cloud Computing

On-demand computing services over a network. Three service models:

  • IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service): Raw compute/storage (e.g., AWS EC2)
  • PaaS (Platform as a Service): Development platform (e.g., Google App Engine)
  • SaaS (Software as a Service): Ready-to-use applications (e.g., Salesforce, Gmail)

Cloud can be public (shared, vendor-managed) or private (dedicated, firm-managed). A hybrid cloud combines both. Drawbacks: security concerns, reliability/uptime dependency on vendor.

Green Computing

Practices for environmentally responsible manufacturing, using, and disposing of computing hardware. Includes energy-efficient processors and data centers.

Autonomic Computing

Industry-wide effort to build systems that can self-configure, self-heal, self-protect (like antivirus auto-updates). Reduces need for human IT management.

💻
Contemporary Software Platform Trends

Open-Source Software

Produced by a community of programmers, free to use and modify. Key examples: Apache (web server), Firefox (browser), OpenOffice (productivity), Linux (OS). Linux runs on mobile devices, servers, and supercomputers.

Java & HTML5

Java: Object-oriented, OS-independent programming language — "write once, run anywhere." Used for web apps and enterprise systems.

HTML5: Latest web page language evolution. Adds native animation and video (previously required Flash or other plugins).

Web Services & XML

Web services are software components that exchange information using standard web protocols. They allow different applications (even from different vendors) to communicate.

XML (Extensible Markup Language) is more powerful than HTML — it uses tags that computers can process automatically to understand meaning, not just display.

HTML says "make this text bold." XML says "this text IS a price" — so a computer can add it up, compare it, or process it without human help.

SOA (Service-Oriented Architecture)

A software design approach where applications are built from reusable, self-contained services that communicate with each other. One "invoice service" can be used by multiple departments instead of building separate billing systems.

Example: Dollar Rent A Car uses web services to link its booking system with Southwest Airlines' website — without building a custom connection to each airline.

Software Outsourcing & Cloud Services

Firms get software from three external sources:

  • Software packages: Off-the-shelf enterprise software (SAP, Oracle)
  • Software outsourcing: Contracting outside firms to develop custom software
  • SaaS (Cloud-based): Accessed via web browser; governed by SLAs (Service Level Agreements)

Mashups combine two or more online applications (e.g., Google Maps + local business data). Apps are small software pieces running on internet, PCs, or mobile devices (iOS, Android).

Management Issues

Scalability

Infrastructure must be flexible and scalable — able to expand as the firm grows. Cloud computing and mobile platforms require new policies for management and contracting.

Management & Governance

  • Centralized IT: Central IT department makes all decisions — consistent standards, but less flexibility
  • Decentralized IT: Each business unit controls its own IT — more flexibility, but less coordination

Total Cost of Ownership (TCO)

TCO measures ALL costs of an IT asset — direct and indirect. Crucially: hardware and software are only ~20% of TCO. The remaining 80% includes:

  • Installation, training, support, maintenance
  • Infrastructure, downtime, space, energy

TCO can be reduced through cloud services, centralization, and standardization of hardware/software.

Competitive Forces Model for IT Investment

Six factors determine how much to spend on IT infrastructure:

  • 1. Market demand for the firm's services
  • 2. Firm's business strategy
  • 3. Firm's IT strategy, infrastructure & cost
  • 4. Information technology assessment
  • 5. Competitor firm services
  • 6. Competitor firm IT infrastructure investments

Common Exam Traps ⚠️

  • Hardware + Software costs = only ~20% of TCO — NOT the majority
  • Moore's Law = 18 months (NOT 12, NOT 24)
  • Mass Storage Law = 1 year double (NOT 18 months — that's Moore's)
  • Server OS: Linux/Unix = 65% — NOT Windows (Windows dominates clients at 90%)
  • Cloud = fastest growing, but has security + reliability drawbacks
  • IaaS ≠ PaaS. IaaS = raw servers. PaaS = development environment.
  • Metcalfe's Law = value grows exponentially, not linearly
  • SOA = design architecture (NOT a vendor product)
  • First magnetic storage device = 1955, first IBM PC = 1981, first IBM mainframe = 1958
